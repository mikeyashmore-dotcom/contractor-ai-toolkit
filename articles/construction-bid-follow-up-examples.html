Construction Bid Follow-Up: What to Say (With Examples)

The polite nudges that win jobs one-and-done bidders lose.

Most estimators send a bid and go silent. The one who follows up — politely, on a schedule — is the one who gets the call back. It's not about being pushy. It's about being the contractor who's easy to work with and still on the GC's radar when they're ready to award.

Here's a simple three-touch sequence you can copy.

When to follow up

  • Day 3 — a friendly check-in
  • Day 7 — a value nudge
  • Day 14 — a polite close-out

The three messages

Day 3 — the friendly check-in
Hi [Name], just checking in on the [project] bid I sent Tuesday. Happy to walk through any of it or adjust the scope if that helps. Does the timeline still line up on your end?
Day 7 — the value nudge
Hi [Name], following up on my [project] quote. If it's helpful, I can break out the pricing by area or suggest a spec that saves a little on material. Just let me know — I'd like to help you make this one work.
Day 14 — the polite close-out
Hi [Name], I'll close out the [project] file on my end unless it's still live for you. No pressure either way — if it comes back around, I'm glad to pick it up. Appreciate the chance to bid it.

Why this works

  • Short. A busy GC reads the first line and decides. Don't write a paragraph.
  • Helpful, not needy. Every message offers something — answers, a price break, flexibility — instead of just "any update?"
  • Spaced out. Three touches over two weeks is professional. Daily pestering is not.

Frequently asked questions

Should I follow up by call, text, or email?

Match how the GC already talks to you. If they text, text. A quick, friendly message in their preferred channel beats a formal email they'll ignore.

What if they still don't respond after three touches?

Close it out gracefully and keep them on your list. A "no" today is often a "yes" on the next project — and the contractor who stayed polite gets that call.
