How to Do a Construction Takeoff (Beginner's Guide)

The foundation of every accurate estimate — for any trade.

Every estimate starts with a takeoff. Get the takeoff right and the rest of your bid is just pricing. Get it wrong and you're either leaving money on the table or eating the difference. Here's how it works, start to finish.

What is a takeoff?

A takeoff is measuring a set of plans to figure out exactly how much material and labor a job needs. It's called a "takeoff" because you're taking the quantities off the drawings. It's the quantities only — pricing comes after.

Step 1 — Read the plans and set your scale

Before you measure anything, confirm the drawing scale (e.g. 1/4" = 1'). If you're measuring on-screen from a PDF, set the scale in your viewer using a known dimension. Measure at the wrong scale and every number is wrong.

Step 2 — Identify your scope

Only measure what you're responsible for. If you're the drywall sub, you don't count the concrete. Mark up the plans or use layers so you don't double-count or miss an area.

Step 3 — Measure by type

  • Area (square feet) — walls, ceilings, flooring, roofing, insulation
  • Linear (feet) — trim, baseboard, pipe, conduit
  • Count (each) — fixtures, outlets, doors, windows

Keep each type in its own column so your material list stays clean.

Step 4 — Add a waste factor

Materials get cut, broken, and mismeasured. Add a waste factor — usually 5–15% depending on the material and layout — so you order enough. Always keep it as a separate line so you can see it.

Don't trust AI with these numbers. AI is great for organizing and writing, but bad at arithmetic. Use a real calculator for the quantities. The free BuildTally calculators (concrete, drywall, flooring, insulation, paint, roofing) run the actual formulas and even have a Waste Factors Explained guide.

Step 5 — Build the material list

Turn your measurements into an organized list ready for pricing. This is a great spot for AI: paste your raw numbers and have it format a clean list (without inventing quantities). Then you apply unit costs, labor, and markup — that's your estimate.

Common beginner mistakes

  • Measuring at the wrong scale
  • Forgetting the waste factor
  • Double-counting overlapping areas
  • Missing items on the second page of a spec
  • Letting a chatbot do the math

Frequently asked questions

How long does a takeoff take?

Anywhere from a few minutes for a small residential job to hours for a large commercial set. On-screen tools and calculators cut it down a lot.

Do I need special software?

Not to start — plans, a scale, and a calculator work. But dedicated takeoff software reduces errors and speeds up big jobs.
